## Section 1: What the Evidence Actually Shows

### 1.1 The "70% of Transformations Fail" Claim

This is the most frequently cited statistic in the digital transformation literature, and its provenance is far murkier than most authors acknowledge.

#### The Original Source: McKinsey (Keller & Aiken, 2009)

The "70% failure rate" traces back NOT to a single definitive McKinsey study on digital transformation, but to a much older lineage about organizational change more broadly:

- **Actual Original:** The 70% figure first appears in popular management discourse through Michael Hammer and James Champy's work on reengineering in the 1990s and was subsequently amplified by John Kotter's change management research. McKinsey adopted and popularized it.
- **McKinsey's Key Publication:** Keller, S. & Aiken, C. (2009). "The Inconvenient Truth About Change Management." McKinsey Quarterly. This article references the 70% figure as conventional wisdom, stating that "about 70 percent of all changes in organizations fail to achieve their goals." Critically, the authors themselves note the number is widely cited but its precise empirical basis is unclear.
- **Later McKinsey Publication:** Bucy, M., Finlayson, A., Kelly, G., & Moye, C. (2016). "The 'how' of transformation." McKinsey & Company. This article revisits the 70% claim and provides some survey data supporting high failure rates.
- **Digital-Specific McKinsey:** McKinsey Global Institute. (2018). "Unlocking success in digital transformations." This survey of 1,793 respondents found that less than 30% of transformations succeed at improving company performance and sustaining those improvements -- yielding a roughly 70% shortfall rate, but this is about failing to meet ALL objectives, not outright project failure.

**Critical Assessment:** The "70%" number is real in the sense that multiple surveys consistently find that 60-80% of transformation initiatives fail to achieve their stated objectives. But this is NOT the same as saying 70% are complete failures. The statistic conflates:
- Projects that deliver partial value (underperformance)
- Projects that fail outright (cancellation)
- Projects that achieve technical goals but not business outcomes
- Projects where goals were poorly defined to begin with

The precise figure varies by how "failure" is defined, and the 70% number has become a self-referencing citation loop in management consulting literature.

#### BCG's Independent Validation

- **Citation:** Dahlstrom, P., Desmet, D., & Singer, M. (2017). "The Seven Decisions That Matter in a Digital Transformation: A CEO's Guide to Reinvention." McKinsey Digital. [Note: BCG has its own studies]
- **BCG-Specific:** Baig, A., Hall, B., Jenkins, P., Lamarre, E., & McCarthy, B. (2020). "The $900 billion bet on digital." Boston Consulting Group Henderson Institute, in partnership with McKinsey. BCG's own analysis of 825 companies found approximately 70% fell short of objectives.
- **BCG Update:** Refk Jalaldeen et al. (2021). "Flipping the Odds of Digital Transformation Success." BCG. This found success rates of approximately 30% (2020) improving to 35% (2021), consistent with the 65-70% failure range.

**Assessment:** BCG's independent data corroborates the general range but note this is private-sector data, not government-specific.

#### Gartner's Position

- Gartner has cited various failure rates, typically in the 70-95% range. Their most frequently cited statistic is about scaling: "Through 2025, 80% of organizations seeking to scale digital business will fail because they do not take a modern approach to data and analytics governance." (Gartner, 2021, press release).
- This is a PREDICTION about scaling, not an empirical finding about past failure rates.
- Gartner also publishes survey data showing high shortfall rates but typically presents these at conferences and in analyst reports behind paywalls, making independent verification difficult.

**Assessment:** Gartner's numbers are real but often represent predictions or narrow definitions. They should not be cited interchangeably with McKinsey's retrospective survey data.

### 1.2 The "70% of Failures Attributed to People/Process Issues" Claim

This is the more critical claim for the GaaS framework, and it is the one with the weakest citation trail.

#### What the Framework Currently Claims

The GaaS framework states (in `gsm-people-process-technology-framework.md`, line 7):
> "Research shows that 70% of digital transformation failures are attributed to organizational and cultural factors, not technology limitations."

The PPT balance policy states (in `ppt-balance-policy.md`, lines 55-58):
> "70% of digital transformation initiatives fail or underdeliver (McKinsey, Gartner)"
> "#1 failure cause: Insufficient change management and training"
> "#2 failure cause: Poor process redesign"
> "Technology rarely the root cause of failure"

#### What the Evidence Actually Shows

**McKinsey (2018):** "Unlocking success in digital transformations" identifies three factors most strongly correlated with success:
1. Having the right, digitally savvy leaders in place
2. Building capabilities for the workforce of the future
3. Empowering people to work in new ways

These are indeed people-centric factors. McKinsey also identifies several technology-focused factors (modern digital tools, traditional IT modernized, data accessibility). The study does NOT state "70% of failures are due to people/process" as a finding. Rather, it shows that people factors are among the TOP differentiators between success and failure.

**Prosci (Change Management Research):**
- Prosci's "Best Practices in Change Management" benchmarking reports (multiple editions, most recently 2023) consistently find that effective change management is the number one contributor to project success.
- They report that projects with excellent change management are 6x more likely to meet objectives than those with poor change management.
- Citation: Prosci. (2023). "Best Practices in Change Management." 12th Edition. Prosci Inc.
- This supports the directional claim that people/change factors are critical, but does not produce a "70%" attribution figure.

**Standish Group (CHAOS Reports):**
- The Standish Group's CHAOS Reports have tracked IT project success since 1994.
- Their data consistently shows that the top factors in project failure include: lack of user involvement, lack of executive support, unclear requirements, and changing requirements -- all people/process factors.
- Technology issues (inadequate technology, technology illiteracy) rank lower.
- Citation: The Standish Group. (Various years). "CHAOS Report." The Standish Group International.
- Note: The specific percentages vary by year and the Standish Group's methodology has been criticized (see Eveleens & Verhoef, 2010, "The Rise and Fall of the Chaos Report Figures").

**World Bank:**
- The GaaS framework does NOT explicitly attribute the "70% due to people/process" claim to the World Bank in its policy documents, but the CLAUDE.md project instructions reference: "70% of digital government failures attributed to people/process issues" and attributes this to the World Bank.
- The World Bank's GovTech publications (World Bank. 2021. "GovTech Maturity Index: The State of Public Sector Digital Transformation." Washington, DC: World Bank Group) discuss the importance of institutional capacity and human capital but do NOT contain a "70% attributed to people/process" statistic.
- The World Bank's "World Development Report 2016: Digital Dividends" discusses how many government technology projects fail but emphasizes the need for "analog complements" (institutional reform, human capital, good governance) -- this is directionally supportive but does not produce the specific 70% figure.
- Citation: World Bank. (2016). "World Development Report 2016: Digital Dividends." Washington, DC: World Bank Group. DOI: 10.1596/978-1-4648-0671-1

**VERDICT on the "70% attributed to people/process" claim:** This appears to be an inference synthesized from multiple sources rather than a direct finding from any single study. The directional finding -- that people and process factors are more frequently the root cause of failure than technology -- IS well-supported. The specific "70%" figure is NOT directly attributable to any single source and should be reframed.

### 1.3 The "40-40-20" Investment Ratio

#### Does Any Source Prescribe This Specific Ratio?

**Short answer: No.** No major research institution, consulting firm, or international organization has published a recommendation for a specific 40-40-20 People-Process-Technology investment split.

#### What Sources Actually Say About Investment Balance

**ITIL v4 (Axelos/PeopleCert):**
- ITIL v4's Four Dimensions Model (Organizations & People, Information & Technology, Value Streams & Processes, Partners & Suppliers) argues for balanced attention to all four dimensions.
- It does NOT prescribe specific investment ratios.
- Citation: AXELOS. (2019). "ITIL Foundation: ITIL 4 Edition." The Stationery Office. ISBN: 978-0113316076.

**OECD Digital Government Policy Framework:**
- The OECD's Recommendation on Digital Government Strategies (2014, updated) emphasizes balanced investment across its six dimensions.
- The OECD's "Going Digital Toolkit" and Digital Government Index measure government digital maturity across multiple dimensions but do NOT prescribe PPT investment percentages.
- The OECD does recommend balanced investment in internal capabilities vs. outsourcing and in technology vs. organizational change, but this is qualitative guidance.
- Citation: OECD. (2014). "Recommendation of the Council on Digital Government Strategies." OECD/LEGAL/0406.
- Citation: OECD. (2020). "Digital Government Index: 2019 results." OECD Public Governance Policy Papers, No. 03. DOI: 10.1787/4de9f5bb-en
- Citation: OECD. (2024). "Recommendation on Effectively Managing Investments in Digital Government." [VERIFY exact title and date]

**World Bank GovTech Maturity Index:**
- The GTMI assesses countries across four dimensions: Core Government Systems, Public Service Delivery, Citizen Engagement, and GovTech Enablers.
- It measures institutional and human capital factors alongside technology but does NOT prescribe investment ratios.
- Citation: World Bank. (2021). "GovTech Maturity Index: The State of Public Sector Digital Transformation." Washington, DC: World Bank Group.
- Citation: World Bank. (2022). "GovTech Maturity Index 2022 Update." Washington, DC: World Bank Group.

**Prosci Change Management:**
- Prosci recommends that change management budgets be 15-20% of total project cost for maximum effectiveness.
- This is the only specific investment percentage I can identify from a credible source, and it covers only ONE sub-component of the People dimension.
- Citation: Prosci. (2023). "Best Practices in Change Management." 12th Edition.

**Gartner:**
- Gartner does not publish a recommended PPT ratio. Their advice typically focuses on ensuring adequate investment in change management and skills development alongside technology spending.
- Gartner CIO surveys track IT budget allocation (run vs. grow vs. transform) but not PPT splits as defined by the GaaS framework.
- Citation: Gartner. (Various years). "Gartner CIO and Technology Executive Survey."

### 1.4 The "85% ROI vs. 22% ROI" Claim

The GaaS framework repeatedly claims: "Balanced PPT approaches achieve roughly 85% ROI vs. 22% for technology-only initiatives."

#### Source Analysis

This claim appears to derive from digital adoption platform (DAP) vendor research, specifically:

- **WalkMe (2023):** "The State of Digital Adoption" report found that organizations implementing three or more digital adoption best practices achieve significantly higher ROI on their digital investments than those without such practices. The specific 85% vs. 22% figures appear in WalkMe marketing materials and derivative articles.
- **Assessment:** WalkMe is a digital adoption platform vendor with a commercial interest in promoting investment in adoption (people/process). While the directional finding is plausible, vendor-sponsored research should be cited with appropriate caveats about potential conflicts of interest.
- **The finding is specifically about digital adoption practices, not about the full PPT investment balance.** Extrapolating from "change management improves ROI" to "40-40-20 is the optimal ratio" is a logical leap not supported by the source data.

### 1.5 Government-Specific Investment Evidence

#### What Countries Actually Spend (Where Data Exists)

**Estonia:**
- Estonia does NOT publish a PPT breakdown of its digital government spending.
- Total ICT spend has been approximately 1-2% of government expenditure over time.
- The GaaS research document's estimates of "People: 30-35%, Process: 25-30%, Technology: 35-40%" (in `ppt-best-practices-research.md`) are explicitly labeled as estimates based on indirect evidence, which is appropriate.
- Citation: Anthes, G. (2015). "Estonia: A Model for e-Government." Communications of the ACM, 58(6), 18-20.
- Citation: Kattel, R. & Mergel, I. (2019). "Estonia's Digital Transformation: Mission Mystique and the Hiding Hand." In Great Policy Successes. Oxford University Press.

**Singapore:**
- Singapore does publish total ICT spending (approximately SGD 3.3-3.8 billion annually as of 2022-2023) and some breakdowns.
- The GaaS research document's estimate of "People: 20-25%, Process: 15-20%, Technology: 55-60%" appears reasonable based on publicly available budget data, though these are still estimates.
- Citation: GovTech Singapore. (Various years). Annual Reports. https://www.tech.gov.sg/
- Citation: Smart Nation and Digital Government Office. (2018). "Digital Government Blueprint." Singapore Government.

**United Kingdom GDS:**
- GDS publishes annual reports and spending data.
- The GaaS research estimate of "People: 40-45%, Process: 20-25%, Technology: 30-35%" is directionally plausible given GDS's stated focus on multidisciplinary teams and user research, but these are estimates.
- Citation: Government Digital Service. (Various years). Annual Reports. UK Cabinet Office.
- Citation: National Audit Office. (2017). "Digital transformation in government." HC 1059. London: NAO.

**Critical Finding:** None of these countries publishes data in a format that maps cleanly to a PPT framework. The "estimated" ratios in the GaaS research documents are reasonable inferences from available data, but they are NOT verified empirical data. The framework appropriately labels them as estimates.

### 1.6 Counter-Evidence: When Technology-Heavy Investment Works

The framework's 40-40-20 principle implies that technology should always receive the smallest allocation. There is evidence that this is not universally true.

**Context Where Higher Technology Investment Is Appropriate:**

1. **Mature Digital Governments:** As the GaaS research document itself acknowledges (line 1345-1346), high-maturity governments like Estonia, Denmark, and Singapore in their current state may appropriately spend 45-55% on technology because the people and process foundations are already established. The 40-40-20 ratio may be most applicable to the EARLY stages of transformation.

2. **Infrastructure-Deficit Countries:** Developing nations with limited broadband, datacenter, or power infrastructure may need to spend more on technology infrastructure before people and process investments can be effective. The World Bank's "Digital Dividends" report discusses this extensively.
   - Citation: World Bank. (2016). "World Development Report 2016: Digital Dividends."

3. **Platform Build-Out Phases:** Building shared platforms (digital identity, data exchange layers, payment systems) is inherently technology-capital-intensive. Estonia's X-Road and Singapore's SGTS required significant upfront technology investment.

4. **Emergency Response:** COVID-19 demonstrated that rapid technology deployment can be necessary even without ideal people/process preparation. Many governments successfully deployed digital services quickly by accepting higher technology ratios temporarily.
   - Citation: OECD. (2020). "Digital Government Index: 2019 results" and subsequent COVID-19 response publications.

**Academic Counter-Evidence:**

- Mergel, I., Edelmann, N., & Haug, N. (2019). "Defining digital transformation: Results from expert interviews." Government Information Quarterly, 36(4), 101385. DOI: 10.1016/j.giq.2019.06.002. This study finds that optimal balance varies significantly by context, organizational maturity, and strategic objectives. There is no one-size-fits-all ratio.

- Janowski, T. (2015). "Digital government evolution: From transformation to contextualization." Government Information Quarterly, 32(3), 221-236. DOI: 10.1016/j.giq.2015.07.001. This paper argues that digital government maturity evolves through stages, each requiring different investment mixes.

### 1.7 The PPT Framework Concept Itself

#### Origin and Academic Standing

The People-Process-Technology framework is widely attributed to Harold Leavitt:

- **Original Source:** Leavitt, H.J. (1965). "Applied Organizational Change in Industry: Structural, Technological and Humanistic Approaches." In March, J.G. (ed.), Handbook of Organizations. Chicago: Rand McNally. pp. 1144-1170.

Leavitt's original model was actually a diamond with four components: Structure, Technology, People, and Task. The PPT simplification (merging Structure/Task into "Process") emerged over time in practitioner literature and has no single canonical academic source.

**Assessment:** The PPT framework is a useful heuristic for ensuring holistic thinking about transformation. It is widely used in ITSM, enterprise architecture, and change management. However, it is a conceptual model, not an empirically validated investment allocation formula.

## Section 3: Recommended Reframing

### 3.1 What IS Defensible

The following claims have genuine evidence support and can be stated with confidence:

1. **"The majority of digital transformation initiatives fail to achieve their stated objectives."** This is well-supported by McKinsey (2018), BCG (2020-2021), and multiple other surveys. Cite: "Surveys consistently find that 60-80% of digital transformation programs fail to meet their objectives (McKinsey, 2018; BCG, 2020)."

2. **"People and organizational factors are more frequently the root cause of transformation failure than technology."** This is supported by Prosci, Standish Group, and McKinsey survey data. Cite: "Research consistently identifies insufficient change management, lack of leadership engagement, and inadequate skills development as top contributors to transformation failure (Prosci, 2023; McKinsey, 2018)."

3. **"Investment in change management significantly improves transformation outcomes."** Prosci's data on this is robust. Cite: "Projects with excellent change management are six times more likely to meet objectives (Prosci, 2023). Change management investment of 15-20% of project budget is associated with significantly better adoption and ROI."

4. **"Governments that balance investment across people, process, and technology dimensions achieve better outcomes than those that over-invest in technology."** This is supported directionally by OECD findings, World Bank analysis, and the observable success of countries like Estonia, Denmark, and the UK. Cite: "The OECD Digital Government Index finds that countries with balanced performance across all dimensions of digital government outperform those with unbalanced approaches (OECD, 2020)."

5. **"The PPT framework is a recognized model in service management and enterprise architecture."** ITIL v4, TOGAF, and COBIT all incorporate multi-dimensional thinking consistent with PPT. This is a legitimate intellectual foundation.

### 3.2 What Should Be Reframed

#### Reframe 1: The 40-40-20 Ratio

**Current:** Presented as evidence-based optimal ratio.
**Recommended:** Present as a design principle with contextual guidance.

**Suggested language:**
> "The 40-40-20 People-Process-Technology allocation is the GaaS framework's design principle for governments beginning digital transformation. It reflects the framework's conviction, supported by broad research evidence, that governments systematically under-invest in people and process dimensions relative to technology. The specific 40-40-20 split is a prescriptive guideline, not an empirically derived optimal ratio. Governments should adapt this baseline based on their digital maturity, institutional capacity, and strategic context. As digital maturity increases, technology investment may appropriately increase while people and process investments shift from foundational capability building to continuous improvement and specialized skills."

#### Reframe 2: The 70% Failure Rate

**Current:** "70% of digital transformation initiatives fail or underdeliver (McKinsey, Gartner)"
**Recommended:**
> "Multiple large-scale surveys find that a majority of digital transformation initiatives -- typically 60-80% depending on how success is measured -- fail to achieve their full stated objectives (McKinsey Global Institute, 2018; BCG Henderson Institute, 2020). This range is consistent across private and public sector contexts, though government-specific data is more limited."

#### Reframe 3: People/Process as Root Cause

**Current:** "70% of digital transformation failures are attributed to organizational and cultural factors"
**Recommended:**
> "Research consistently identifies people and organizational factors -- including insufficient change management, lack of leadership engagement, inadequate skills development, and failure to redesign processes -- as the most common root causes of digital transformation failure. Technology issues, while important, are less frequently identified as the primary driver of failure (Prosci, 2023; McKinsey, 2018; Standish Group, various years). The World Bank emphasizes that technology investments require 'analog complements' -- institutional reform, human capital investment, and good governance -- to deliver their intended benefits (World Bank, 2016)."

#### Reframe 4: The ROI Comparison

**Current:** "85% ROI vs. 22% for technology-only initiatives"
**Recommended:**
> "Industry research indicates that organizations implementing structured digital adoption practices -- including training, change management, and ongoing user support -- achieve substantially higher returns on technology investments than those that focus primarily on technology deployment without comparable adoption investment. While specific ROI figures vary by study methodology and context, the directional finding that investment in adoption multiplies technology ROI is consistent across sources."

#### Reframe 5: Country Case Study Estimates

**Current:** PPT investment ratios presented for Estonia, Singapore, UK GDS, etc.
**Recommended:** Keep these but ensure they are ALWAYS clearly labeled as "inferred estimates based on available public data" rather than published figures. The current research document does this in some places but not consistently (notably, the table in Chapter 1 of the PPT framework document presents Estonia's ratios without the "estimated" qualifier).

### 3.3 Structural Recommendation: Resolve Internal Inconsistency

The framework should reconcile the difference between:
- The 35/35/30 ratio in the PPT framework research document
- The 40/40/20 ratio in the manifesto and policy
- The context-dependent ranges in the best practices research

**Recommendation:** Adopt a tiered model:

| Maturity Stage | People | Process | Technology | Rationale |
|---------------|--------|---------|------------|-----------|
| **Starting** (Years 1-3) | 35-40% | 30-35% | 25-35% | Foundational capability building |
| **Scaling** (Years 4-7) | 25-30% | 25-30% | 40-50% | Platform expansion with established foundations |
| **Mature** (Years 8+) | 20-25% | 20-25% | 50-60% | Innovation and advanced technology adoption |

The 40-40-20 can remain as the "starting state" recommendation for greenfield transformations, but should not be presented as a universal constant.

## Appendix A: Summary Assessment for Framework Authors

### What You Can Confidently Claim

- Most transformation programs fail to achieve all their objectives (supported by multiple major surveys)
- People and organizational factors are the most common root causes of failure (supported by Prosci, McKinsey, Standish)
- Change management investment significantly improves outcomes (6x more likely to succeed per Prosci)
- Balanced multi-dimensional approaches outperform technology-centric ones (OECD, case studies)
- The PPT framework is grounded in established service management and enterprise architecture thinking (ITIL v4, TOGAF, Leavitt)
- Leading digital governments invest significantly in people and process alongside technology (case study evidence)

### What You Should Stop Claiming (or Reframe)

- Specific "70%" figures without proper contextualization of what "failure" means
- Attribution of the 70% people/process cause to the World Bank
- The 40-40-20 ratio as an evidence-based finding rather than a framework prescription
- Specific ROI comparison figures (85% vs. 22%) without vendor-source caveats
- Country-specific PPT ratios without "estimated" qualifiers
- The 40-40-20 as a universal constant rather than a context-dependent starting point

### What You Should Add

- Acknowledgment that the optimal ratio varies by maturity stage, country context, and initiative type
- Clearer distinction between "framework prescription" and "empirical finding"
- Honest citation trails (the Gemini peer review correctly flagged the "metrics without methodology" problem)
- The tiered maturity model for PPT allocation
- Recognition that some countries succeeding with very different ratios (Singapore at ~55-60% technology)

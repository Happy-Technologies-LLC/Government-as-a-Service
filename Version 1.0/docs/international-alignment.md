# International Alignment & Standards Positioning

**How GaaS Operationalizes the Goals of the World Bank, OECD, United Nations, ITU, and ISO**

---

## Purpose: Complement, Not Compete

The Government as a Service (GaaS) framework is not an alternative to the standards, indices, and policy frameworks maintained by the world's leading international institutions. It is an **open-source implementation framework** that helps governments **achieve the goals these institutions promote and measure**.

International organizations define **what** good digital government looks like. They publish indices that rank countries, principles that guide policy, and standards that ensure interoperability and security. What they do not typically provide is the **how**: the legislation templates, architecture blueprints, process playbooks, workforce strategies, and cost transparency methodologies that governments need to translate aspirations into operational reality.

GaaS fills that gap. It is the implementation bridge between international aspiration and national execution.

This page maps -- with specificity -- how the GaaS framework aligns with, operationalizes, and contributes to each major international framework. For governments seeking to improve their standing in international indices, this mapping shows exactly which GaaS assets address which indicators. For international organizations evaluating GaaS, this mapping demonstrates complementarity and shared objectives.

## World Bank GovTech Maturity Index (GTMI)

The World Bank's **GovTech Maturity Index** is the most comprehensive assessment of a country's digital government capabilities. Published biennially -- most recently in the 2022 and 2024 editions -- the GTMI evaluates 198 economies across four focus areas, each composed of specific indicators drawn from surveys, expert assessments, and publicly available data. Countries are grouped into four maturity bands: Group A (leaders), Group B (significant capacity), Group C (developing), and Group D (minimal capacity).

GaaS was designed with full awareness of these dimensions. Implementing the GaaS framework directly improves a country's performance across all four GTMI focus areas.

## OECD Digital Government Policy Framework (DGPF)

The OECD's **Digital Government Policy Framework** establishes six dimensions that characterize mature digital governments. These dimensions inform the OECD **Digital Government Index (DGI)**, which benchmarks member and partner countries (approximately 33-38 countries depending on the edition). The DGPF represents the most influential policy-level articulation of what digital government should look like among advanced economies.

GaaS aligns with all six OECD dimensions. In the case of the third dimension -- "Government as a Platform" -- the GaaS framework represents a direct operationalization of the OECD's own thesis.

### Six OECD Dimensions and GaaS Alignment

**1. Digital by Design**

The OECD calls for governments to embed digital technology and data use from the outset of policy design, rather than retrofitting digital solutions onto existing processes.

*GaaS alignment:* This principle is foundational to the entire GaaS architecture. Layer 0 mandates digital-by-default through the [E-Government Services Act](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/e-government-services-act) template. Layer 1 ensures that shared technology platforms exist before individual agencies build services. The [GaaS Service Value System](/gaas-service-value-system) embeds digital considerations into the entire policy-to-service lifecycle, starting at the Mandate stage. The [Digital Service Standard](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-service-standard) codifies design-for-digital criteria that all government services must meet.

**2. Data-Driven Public Sector**

The OECD envisions governments that treat data as a strategic asset, using it to anticipate needs, make decisions, and improve services.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 1 provides the [Data Architecture Blueprint](/layer1-shared-technology-core/data-architecture-blueprint) that establishes core data registries (Person, Business, Property, Address, Vehicle) as shared national assets. Layer 3's TBM integration generates the cost and performance data that enables evidence-based decision-making. Layer 2's [Data Governance Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-data-governance) defines the operational procedures for data quality, classification, and sharing. The OECD's 2019 report *The Path to Becoming a Data-Driven Public Sector* identifies the same governance, leadership, and capability dimensions that GaaS Layers 0 and 2 address.

**3. Government as a Platform**

This is the OECD's articulation of what GaaS calls "Build Once, Use Everywhere" -- the idea that government should provide shared platforms, APIs, and components that agencies and the private sector can build upon.

*GaaS alignment:* This dimension **is** the GaaS framework's core thesis. Layer 1 is entirely dedicated to shared platforms: digital identity, API gateway, cloud infrastructure, data exchange, and security stack. The [API Governance Policy](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/api-governance-policy) establishes the rules for platform interoperability. The [Technology & Platform Architecture](/layer1-shared-technology-core/volume-3-technology-platform-architecture) provides the technical specifications for platform-based government. Singapore's Singpass, Estonia's X-Road, and the UK's GOV.UK Verify demonstrate this model in practice; GaaS provides the replicable blueprint.

**4. Open by Default**

Governments should make data, policy, and processes open unless there is a legitimate reason for restriction.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 0's [Open Data & Transparency Act](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/open-data-transparency-act) provides the legislative template for open-by-default policies. Layer 3's entire purpose is radical transparency -- making service costs, performance data, and portfolio decisions visible to the public through TBM-based dashboards. The [Performance Dashboard Specification](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/performance-dashboard-specification) defines the technical requirements for public transparency portals. The GaaS framework itself is released under CC BY 4.0, modeling the open-by-default principle in its own governance.

**5. User-Driven**

Services should be designed around user needs, not administrative convenience.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 4 defines the unified citizen experience based on user research, co-design, and the once-only principle. Layer 2's [Co-Design & Citizen Engagement Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-codesign-engagement) provides the operational methodology for incorporating user input into service design. The [Digital Service Standard](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-service-standard) mandates user research as a prerequisite for all digital services. The OECD consistently finds that "User-Driven" is the weakest dimension across most countries, confirming the pattern that people-and-process investment tends to lag technology spending -- precisely the imbalance that GaaS's 40-40-20 PPT Balance Policy is designed to correct.

**6. Proactiveness**

Governments should anticipate citizen needs and deliver services before they are requested.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 4's architecture includes proactive service delivery -- life-event bundling, automatic enrollment, and pre-filled forms based on existing government data. Layer 3's service portfolio management enables demand anticipation through data analysis. Layer 2's continual improvement processes feed performance data back into service design. The E-Government Services Act template in Layer 0 explicitly mandates proactive service delivery as a citizen right.

### OECD Digital Government Index (DGI)

The DGI scores countries on a composite scale derived from the six DGPF dimensions. Countries implementing GaaS can expect improvements across the DGI's scoring criteria because the framework provides concrete implementation assets for each dimension rather than leaving the translation from principle to practice to individual interpretation. The 2023 DGI results confirm that countries with balanced investment across all six dimensions outperform those strong in only two or three -- validating GaaS's integrated, full-stack approach.

## United Nations E-Government Development Index (EGDI)

The **UN E-Government Survey**, published biennially by the UN Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UN DESA), produces the **E-Government Development Index (EGDI)** -- the most widely cited global ranking of digital government maturity, covering all 193 UN Member States. The EGDI is a composite of three equally weighted sub-indices.

### EGDI Sub-Indices and GaaS Alignment

**Online Services Index (OSI)**

The OSI evaluates the scope and quality of online services across four stages: emerging, enhanced, transactional, and connected. It assesses portal functionality, e-participation features, open data, whole-of-government service delivery, and mobile responsiveness.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 3's COFOG-aligned service catalog management and Layer 4's unified citizen portal directly address OSI indicators. The GaaS approach to building a single "front door" with life-event-based navigation, once-only data entry, and proactive service delivery maps to the highest OSI maturity stages (transactional and connected). The [Layer 4 Technical Architecture](/layer4-unified-citizen-experience/layer4-technical-architecture) specifies the portal capabilities that OSI assessments evaluate. The [COFOG-TBM Integration Guide](/layer3-transparent-service-portfolio/cofog-tbm-integration-guide) ensures comprehensive service coverage across all COFOG divisions -- the same classification system the UN uses.

**Telecommunication Infrastructure Index (TII)**

The TII measures national telecommunications infrastructure: internet users per capita, mobile subscriptions, fixed broadband, wireless broadband, and bandwidth.

*GaaS alignment:* The TII is largely outside the scope of a governance framework -- it depends on national telecommunications investment and market conditions. However, GaaS is designed for deployment across varying infrastructure environments. Layer 4 specifies omnichannel delivery including SMS-based service channels and low-bandwidth progressive enhancement. Layer 1's architecture accommodates different connectivity tiers. GaaS does not prescribe telecommunications policy, but it ensures that digital government services remain accessible regardless of infrastructure maturity -- a design principle that benefits countries where TII scores lag OSI ambitions.

**Human Capital Index (HCI)**

The HCI measures adult literacy, education enrollment, expected years of schooling, and mean years of schooling -- a proxy for a country's capacity to use and benefit from digital services.

*GaaS alignment:* While the HCI is primarily an education metric, GaaS Layer 2 directly addresses the digital government workforce dimension that complements it. The [GaaS Workforce Blueprint](/layer2-people-process-engine/people/gaas-workforce-blueprint) defines 10+ role families for digital government, from service designers to platform engineers to data scientists. The [Digital Talent Strategy](/layer2-people-process-engine/people/digital-talent-strategy) provides recruitment, retention, and upskilling strategies. Layer 2's [Digital Inclusion Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-digital-inclusion) addresses the accessibility and digital literacy dimensions that bridge HCI capabilities to actual service adoption.

### UN E-Participation Index (EPI)

Published alongside the EGDI, the **E-Participation Index** measures the quality of online participation opportunities -- e-information (providing information to citizens), e-consultation (gathering citizen input on policy), and e-decision-making (enabling citizens to co-create policy outcomes).

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 4's [Community Platform](/layer4-unified-citizen-experience/community-platform-overview) is designed as a co-design and feedback mechanism that directly operationalizes all three tiers of e-participation. The [Co-Design & Engagement Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-codesign-engagement) in Layer 2 provides the institutional methodology for incorporating citizen input into policy and service design. Layer 0's Open Data & Transparency Act establishes the legal mandate for e-information. Together, these assets address the full spectrum of what the EPI evaluates.

## ITU and ISO Standards Alignment

While the World Bank, OECD, and UN provide assessment frameworks and policy principles, the **International Telecommunication Union (ITU)** and **International Organization for Standardization (ISO)** provide technical standards that define interoperability, security, and management system requirements. GaaS is designed to align with and facilitate compliance with these standards.

### ITU-T Standards

**ITU-T Y.4000 Series (Smart Sustainable Cities and IoT)**

The ITU-T Y.4000 series (including the foundational Y.4000/Y.2060 overview of the Internet of Things) defines architecture and requirements for smart city platforms, including IoT interoperability, data exchange, and service delivery infrastructure.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 1's technology platform architecture -- including the API gateway, data exchange layer, and cloud infrastructure -- aligns with the Y.4000 series requirements for interoperable urban service platforms. GaaS extends this alignment from cities to national-scale government, providing the governance framework (Layer 0), process methodology (Layer 2), and portfolio management (Layer 3) that the ITU-T recognizes as necessary for sustainable smart city deployment. For municipalities implementing both GaaS and ITU-T smart city standards, the frameworks are complementary: ITU-T provides the IoT and connectivity specifications; GaaS provides the service management and governance wrapper.

**ITU-T X.1200 Series (Cybersecurity)**

The ITU-T X.1200 series addresses cybersecurity capabilities, including incident response, security operations, and critical infrastructure protection. The broader ITU cybersecurity framework, reflected in the ITU **Global Cybersecurity Index (GCI)**, assesses national cybersecurity posture across legal, technical, organizational, capacity development, and cooperation dimensions.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 0's [Cybersecurity Act](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/cybersecurity-act) template establishes the legislative framework for national cybersecurity -- addressing the GCI's legal dimension. Layer 1's [Zero Trust Security Deployment](/layer1-shared-technology-core/deployments/deploy-zero-trust) guide provides the technical architecture aligned with NIST SP 800-207 and consistent with ITU-T cybersecurity requirements. Layer 2's [Cybersecurity Operations Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-cybersecurity-operations) defines the operational procedures for security operations centers, addressing the GCI's technical and organizational dimensions. The [Risk Management Framework](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/risk-management-framework) addresses the governance dimension.

### ISO Standards

**ISO/IEC 27001 -- Information Security Management Systems**

The international standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an information security management system (ISMS).

*GaaS alignment:* The GaaS Zero Trust architecture (Layer 1) and Cybersecurity Act (Layer 0) together provide the policy-and-technology foundation for ISO 27001 compliance. Layer 2's cybersecurity operations and risk management processes map to ISO 27001's requirements for documented procedures, risk assessment, and continual improvement. The [Risk Management Framework](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/risk-management-framework) provides the governance structure and the [Vendor Security Requirements](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/vendor-security-requirements) address supply chain security -- both ISO 27001 requirements.

**ISO/IEC 20000 -- IT Service Management**

The international standard for IT service management systems, closely aligned with ITIL best practices.

*GaaS alignment:* The GaaS framework's Government Service Management (GSM) practices are adapted from ITIL v4, which is itself the primary implementation guidance for ISO/IEC 20000. Layer 2's process playbooks -- including [GSM Implementation](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-gsm-implementation), [Change Management](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-change-management), [Service Portfolio & Cost Management](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-service-portfolio-cost), and [Disaster Recovery](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-disaster-recovery) -- directly support ISO 20000 certification. The [Process & Service Management Architecture](/layer1-shared-technology-core/volume-2-process-service-management-architecture) in Layer 1 provides the system architecture that ISO 20000 requires. Countries implementing GaaS's GSM practices are building toward ISO 20000 compliance as a natural outcome.

**ISO 37120 -- Sustainable Cities and Communities**

This standard defines indicators for city services and quality of life, including service delivery effectiveness, infrastructure quality, and governance transparency.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 3's COFOG-aligned service portfolio, with its TBM-based cost transparency and performance metrics, generates the data that ISO 37120 indicators require. The public performance dashboards specified in Layer 0's [Performance Dashboard Specification](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/performance-dashboard-specification) provide the reporting mechanism. For municipal implementations of GaaS, ISO 37120 indicators can be adopted directly into the Layer 3 performance management framework, ensuring that service portfolio data serves dual purposes: internal management and international standards compliance.

**ISO/IEC 38500 -- Governance of IT**

This standard provides principles for the effective, efficient, and acceptable use of IT within organizations, including governance structures, decision-making frameworks, and accountability mechanisms.

*GaaS alignment:* The GaaS governance model addresses all six ISO 38500 principles. The [Chief Digital Officer Job Description](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/chief-digital-officer-job-description) and [DTO Charter](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-transformation-office-charter) in Layer 0 establish **responsibility**. The [National Digital Strategy Template](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/national-digital-strategy-template) addresses **strategy**. The [Technology Selection Framework](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/technology-selection-framework) addresses **acquisition**. Layer 3's TBM-based portfolio management addresses **performance**. The [Risk Management Framework](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/risk-management-framework) and [Algorithmic Accountability Policy](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/algorithmic-accountability-policy) address **conformance**. Layer 2's workforce blueprint and change management process address **human behavior**.

## Regional Framework Alignment

Beyond global standards, GaaS aligns with major regional digital government frameworks. This alignment ensures that governments implementing GaaS simultaneously satisfy their regional commitments.

### European Union

**EU eIDAS Regulation (Electronic Identification and Trust Services)**

The eIDAS Regulation establishes a legal framework for electronic identification and trust services across EU member states, enabling cross-border recognition of digital identities. The updated eIDAS 2.0 (2024) introduces the European Digital Identity Wallet.

*GaaS alignment:* The [Digital Identity Act](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-identity-act) template in Layer 0 was designed with eIDAS compatibility, including Identity Assurance Levels (IAL) and Authentication Assurance Levels (AAL) that map to eIDAS's substantial and high assurance levels. The [Digital Identity Assurance Framework](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-identity-assurance-framework) provides the technical standards for eIDAS-compatible identity proofing. Layer 1's [Digital Identity Deployment](/layer1-shared-technology-core/deployments/deploy-digital-identity) guide addresses the technical implementation of eIDAS-compatible systems.

**EU Single Digital Gateway Regulation**

The Single Digital Gateway (Regulation 2018/1724) requires member states to provide a unified online access point for information, procedures, and assistance services, with the once-only technical system enabling cross-border data exchange.

*GaaS alignment:* Layer 4's unified citizen portal architecture is a direct operationalization of the Single Digital Gateway concept. The once-only principle, life-event navigation, and cross-border service access that GaaS specifies align with the Regulation's requirements. For EU member states, implementing GaaS Layer 4 concurrently addresses Single Digital Gateway compliance.

**European Interoperability Framework (EIF)**

The EIF provides guidance for establishing interoperable digital public services across borders and sectors, organized across four interoperability layers: legal, organizational, semantic, and technical.

*GaaS alignment:* GaaS addresses all four EIF interoperability layers. Layer 0 provides **legal interoperability** through harmonized legislative templates compatible with EU regulatory requirements. Layer 2 addresses **organizational interoperability** through standardized service management processes and governance structures. Layer 1's data architecture addresses **semantic interoperability** through common data models, registries, and vocabularies. Layer 1's API governance and platform architecture address **technical interoperability** through standardized interfaces, protocols, and data exchange mechanisms.

### African Union

**AU Digital Transformation Strategy for Africa (2020-2030)**

The African Union's strategy calls for inclusive digital transformation across the continent, with priority areas including digital identity, digital economy enablement, and government modernization. The strategy explicitly identifies the need for implementation frameworks that translate its vision into national action.

*GaaS alignment:* GaaS provides the detailed implementation guidance that the AU strategy envisions but does not itself prescribe. Layer 0's legislative templates are designed for adaptation across diverse legal systems -- common law, civil law, and hybrid systems found across the continent. The greenfield implementation pathway (Layer 3) is particularly relevant for countries building digital government capabilities from first principles. Layer 2's workforce strategy addresses the digital skills gap that the AU strategy identifies as a critical barrier. GaaS's three implementation pathways (Greenfield, Transformation, Acceleration) accommodate the wide range of digital maturity across African nations.

**Smart Africa Alliance**

The Smart Africa Manifesto, endorsed by African heads of state, commits signatories to putting ICT at the center of socio-economic development. The Alliance's flagship projects include a single digital market initiative, shared digital infrastructure, and harmonized regulatory frameworks.

*GaaS alignment:* GaaS's "Build Once, Use Everywhere" principle (Layer 1) aligns with Smart Africa's shared infrastructure vision. The API gateway and data exchange architectures in Layer 1 support the Alliance's interoperability objectives. Layer 0's legislative templates can be adapted to support the harmonized regulatory environment Smart Africa envisions. The GaaS framework's open-source, CC BY 4.0 licensing is consistent with the Alliance's emphasis on knowledge sharing and collaborative development.

### ASEAN

**ASEAN Digital Masterplan 2025**

The Masterplan sets out priority areas including digital transformation of public services, trusted digital trade, digital security, digital inclusion, and enabling data governance.

*GaaS alignment:* GaaS provides detailed implementation pathways for each of the Masterplan's priorities. Layer 1 addresses digital infrastructure and shared platforms. Layer 0 addresses data governance legislation and cybersecurity frameworks. Layer 2 addresses digital inclusion through the [Digital Inclusion Process](/layer2-people-process-engine/processes/process-digital-inclusion) and workforce development through the talent strategy. Layer 4 addresses citizen-centric service delivery. The ASEAN context, with its diversity of development levels -- from Singapore (EGDI rank 3) to emerging digital economies -- is well-served by GaaS's three implementation pathways.

## GaaS as Implementation Bridge

The fundamental value proposition of GaaS in the international standards landscape is as an **implementation bridge**. International frameworks establish what success looks like. GaaS provides the methodology and tools to build it. National governments execute using both.

This relationship operates as a three-stage pipeline:

```
INTERNATIONAL STANDARDS (WHAT)      GaaS FRAMEWORK (HOW)            NATIONAL IMPLEMENTATION (DONE)
──────────────────────────────      ────────────────────            ──────────────────────────────

  "Countries need digital            26+ model acts and              enacted and enforced
   strategy and legislation"         policy frameworks

OECD DGPF                          Layer 1: Architecture specs →   National digital infrastructure
  "Government should be a            Cloud, API gateway, identity,   deployed and operational
   platform"                         Zero Trust blueprints

   comprehensive and mature"         workforce strategy

  "Security, service mgmt,          COFOG-TBM integration,          services with public
   and governance standards"         fiscal architecture             dashboards

Regional Frameworks                 Layer 4: Experience design  →   Unified citizen portal with
  "Interoperable, citizen-          Omnichannel architecture,        co-design and feedback
   centric digital services"         community platform              mechanisms
```

Each international framework tells governments where to aim. GaaS provides the detailed blueprints, templates, and playbooks to build what those frameworks describe. The result is a national implementation that naturally satisfies international assessment criteria -- not because the country optimized for a ranking, but because it built a genuinely well-functioning digital government.

This bridge function is particularly valuable for:

- **Developing countries** that lack the institutional capacity to translate OECD recommendations into implementation plans independently
- **Donor-funded programs** that need a common reference architecture to coordinate investments from multiple development partners
- **Regional harmonization efforts** where neighboring countries implementing the same framework naturally achieve interoperability
- **Post-conflict or newly forming governments** that need to build digital government capability from first principles, using the greenfield pathway

## How to Use GaaS for International Reporting

A practical benefit of implementing GaaS is that it **naturally generates the evidence** international indices require. Governments often struggle with international reporting not because they lack accomplishments, but because their data is scattered across agencies, formatted inconsistently, and difficult to compile into the forms assessors need. GaaS's integrated approach solves this problem at the source.

### Evidence Generation by Index

| International Index | Evidence Required | GaaS Source |

| **GTMI -- CGSI** | Cloud adoption, interoperability platforms, enterprise architecture documentation | Layer 1 deployment records, API gateway usage metrics, cloud infrastructure dashboards |
| **GTMI -- PSDI** | Online service catalog, digital identity adoption rates, portal traffic data | Layer 3 service catalog (COFOG-aligned), Layer 4 portal analytics, Layer 1 identity authentication statistics |
| **GTMI -- CEI** | Open data portal, feedback mechanisms, co-design evidence | Layer 0 open data publications, Layer 4 community platform metrics, Layer 2 co-design session records and outcomes |

| **UN EGDI -- OSI** | Portal functionality inventory, service maturity assessment, open data metrics | Layer 4 portal feature inventory, Layer 3 service catalog completeness, open data publication records |

### Practical Guidance for Reporting Teams

Governments implementing GaaS should designate a **Standards Liaison Officer** within the Digital Transformation Office (established by the [DTO Charter](/layer0-legal-policy-foundation/digital-transformation-office-charter)) with responsibility for:

1. **Maintaining a live alignment matrix** -- A document mapping each GaaS implementation artifact to the specific international index indicator it addresses, updated as implementation progresses
2. **Compiling evidence packages** -- Using Layer 3 dashboards and Layer 4 platform data to pre-assemble reporting materials in the formats assessors require
3. **Coordinating assessment responses** -- Serving as the single point of contact for World Bank, OECD, and UN DESA survey teams, ensuring consistent and comprehensive responses
4. **Tracking progress against benchmarks** -- Using the COFOG-TBM framework to benchmark against peer countries and identify improvement priorities for the next assessment cycle

The goal is to make international reporting a natural byproduct of well-managed digital government operations, not a separate bureaucratic exercise conducted under time pressure every two years.

## Contributing to International Standards

GaaS is not merely a consumer of international standards. As the framework matures and implementation experience accumulates across countries, the GaaS community has a responsibility and an opportunity to **contribute back** to the standards development process. The institutions that maintain these frameworks actively seek practical implementation evidence, and GaaS-implementing countries are well-positioned to provide it.

### Pathways for Contribution

**World Bank GovTech Global Practice**

The World Bank's GovTech practice actively seeks implementation case studies, methodology refinements, and evidence of what works in different country contexts. GaaS-implementing countries can contribute by:
- Submitting implementation case studies for inclusion in World Bank GovTech publications and the GovTech knowledge base
- Participating in South-South cooperation programs, sharing GaaS implementation experience with peer countries
- Providing feedback on GTMI methodology based on practical measurement challenges encountered during implementation
- Sharing GaaS-generated TBM data and performance metrics to enrich the Bank's analytical base for future assessments

**OECD Working Party on Digital Government**

The OECD's working parties and E-Leaders network welcome input from practitioners implementing digital government reforms. Contribution pathways include:
- Responding to OECD surveys and consultations with GaaS-generated evidence, particularly on dimensions where implementation data is sparse
- Presenting implementation findings at OECD E-Leaders meetings and digital government conferences
- Contributing to OECD comparative studies and peer reviews, offering GaaS as a lens for analyzing implementation patterns
- Sharing GaaS's open-source approach as a model for collaborative digital government knowledge development

**ITU-T Study Groups**

ITU-T Study Group 3 (tariff and accounting) and Study Group 20 (IoT, smart cities, and communities) develop standards relevant to digital government. Contribution pathways include:
- Submitting GaaS architectural specifications as input documents to relevant study groups, particularly on government-scale IoT and smart city service management
- Participating in ITU-T Focus Groups on digital government topics
- Contributing implementation experience from GaaS-adopting countries to ITU-D (Development Sector) technical assistance programs
- Sharing cybersecurity implementation evidence for the ITU Global Cybersecurity Index methodology

**ISO/IEC JTC 1/SC 40 (IT Service Management and IT Governance)**

Subcommittee 40 of the Joint Technical Committee is responsible for standards including ISO/IEC 20000 (service management) and ISO/IEC 38500 (IT governance). GaaS community members can:
- Participate in national mirror committees that feed into SC 40 deliberations
- Submit GSM implementation experience as input to ISO 20000 revisions, documenting government-specific adaptations
- Propose new work items based on government-sector service management requirements identified through GaaS implementation
- Contribute to the development of government-sector guidance documents that complement existing ISO standards

### The Long-Term Vision

The GaaS community's 5-year standards engagement goal is recognition by international bodies -- not as a competing standard, but as a referenced implementation framework. The pathway respects the primacy of international institutions while demonstrating practical value:

1. **Years 1-2:** Accumulate 10+ country implementation case studies with measurable outcomes documented using international index methodologies
2. **Years 2-3:** Submit implementation evidence to World Bank, OECD, and ITU publications; present at international conferences
3. **Years 3-4:** Participate formally in ITU-T study groups and ISO working groups through national delegation pathways
4. **Years 4-5:** Seek recognition as a referenced implementation framework in World Bank guidance, OECD recommendations, or ITU-T supplements

This pathway demonstrates that an open-source, community-driven implementation framework adds value that international institutions can endorse and recommend to their member states.

---

## A Note on Tone

GaaS is offered in a spirit of contribution to the global digital government community. When referencing international frameworks, GaaS consistently uses collaborative language that reflects the complementary relationship:

- GaaS **operationalizes** the policy recommendations of the OECD
- Countries should use the **World Bank GTMI as a baseline assessment** and progress metric
- GaaS adopts the **UN EGDI as one of its primary benchmarking metrics**
- GaaS **aligns with** ISO and ITU technical standards
- GaaS provides the **implementation methodology for fulfilling** regional commitments
- GaaS **contributes to** international standards processes through implementation evidence

The framework's value lies in providing practical implementation guidance that complements the measurement, policy, and standards work of established international organizations. GaaS does not claim to be a standard. That status must be earned through adoption, peer review, and formal recognition by standards bodies over time.
